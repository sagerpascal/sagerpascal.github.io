A PhD wants all of you. A company wants all of you. Three children want all of you.
That is three hundred per cent of a person and nobody has ever had more than one
hundred. The arithmetic does not resolve. Most of what gets written about this pretends
otherwise, usually by implying that the right calendar app closes the gap.

It does not. What follows is what I actually do, and the part of it I think generalises.

## Hours are the worst available signal

I watch PhD students work extraordinarily hard and get very little out of it. They stay
late, they sleep in the lab, they are visibly exhausted, and after two years the thesis
has barely moved. The hours are real. The output is not.

Somewhere the culture started treating time spent as evidence of seriousness. It is the
least reliable signal available. Effort and progress are only loosely related once you
are past a threshold, and beyond a certain point more hours actively destroy the thing
they are supposed to produce, because tired work has to be redone.

I think about my own workflows constantly, more than I think about most research
questions. Where does the time actually go. Which of these meetings changed a decision.
Which experiment would I run if I could only run one. That sounds joyless written down.
In practice it is the only reason three things fit at all.

## Presence is the lever, not scheduling

The largest gain I have found is not a better calendar. It is being entirely in one
thing at a time.

Watching television with my children is not quality time. Sitting on the floor and
playing with them, with nothing else running in my head, is. The difference is not the
hour. It is whether I am actually there. My kids can tell the difference immediately,
and so can colleagues, and so can a proof.

The research says the same thing in colder language. Switching between tasks leaves an
attention residue that measurably degrades performance on the task you moved to
[[1](#r1)], and interrupted work gets completed faster but at a cost in stress and
error [[2](#r2)]. Half-attention work is not half as good. It is work you will do twice.

So the discipline is not to work more. It is to be unreachable inside whatever block
you are in, including the blocks that are not work.

## Do the thing that is most needed right now

In any hour there is usually one thing that moves the whole system furthest. Some days
that is a paper deadline. Some days it is a customer who is about to make a decision.
Some days it is being at home because something there needs me more than anything at
either desk.

I try to do that one thing and nothing else. This is triage per moment rather than per
calendar, and it means my week rarely looks like the week I planned. I have stopped
treating that as a failure of planning. It is the plan.

## None of this makes the work smaller

I want to be careful here, because this is where advice of this kind usually starts
lying.

A good PhD does not happen on less than roughly eight focused hours a day. Neither does
a company. Optimising your workflow removes waste. It does not remove the work. Anyone
promising you the same result from a four-hour week is selling you something, and what
they are selling is usually the book.

The honest version is narrower and more useful. You cannot reduce the requirement. You
can stop paying for things that do not count.

## Three things is a feature

The obvious objection is that I should drop one and go faster. I do not think that is
true, and the reason is a machine learning one.

A model trained on a single narrow distribution fits it beautifully and fails the
moment the world moves. People do this too. Someone who only does research gets very
good at the internal logic of a subfield and loses the ability to tell whether any of
it matters. The three roles regularise each other. Running a company makes me ruthless
about which research questions are worth the compute. Research keeps the company from
building something merely clever. My children reset the clock on both, every day,
whether I want them to or not.

I should also say the thing that underwrites all of it. I love this work. It is not
something I endure in order to have a career, and I start to miss it when I am away
from it for too long. No method survives contact with work you do not want to do.

## The comforting lie

The most damaging thing people in academia tell each other is that it gets easier
later. After the bachelor's. After the master's. After the PhD. After the first
funding round.

It does not. Performance has a price and the price is time and attention. People who
achieve things pay it at every stage, and the amount does not fall. What changes is
what you are buying with it. The literature on expert performance has been saying this
for thirty years: sustained, effortful, deliberate work is the mechanism rather than a
phase you pass through [[3](#r3)].

So do not hide behind the idea that this is temporary. If you intend to do serious work
for a lifetime, you are not in a sprint that ends. You are choosing a rate of payment
you will keep making, and you should pick one you can still make in twenty years. This
is exactly why I refuse to collapse life into one thing. You can work flat out on a
single objective for years. You cannot do it for a life. A life is a package and the
PhD is a chapter in it, not the point of it.

## What makes it possible

The obvious caveat, and it matters more than any technique above. This works for me
because of the people around me. My wife carries more of it than any paragraph here
admits, and I have colleagues and supervisors who cover for me when one of the three
things spikes.

Not everyone has that. A method that quietly assumes it is not a method. It is a
privilege with a plan attached, and I would rather say so than let this read as though
discipline alone got me here.

## The short version

Pay the price. Reduce it by removing waste, not by pretending the work is smaller than
it is. Put in a great deal of effort, because effort is what produces results. Just do
not waste any of it, and do not spend it all in one place.
